Stage 1 · Figure extraction

Extracting values from biotech charts

Every number that matters in a readout is locked inside a picture. Median PFS, hazard ratios by subgroup, how many patients shrank past 30 percent: published as a chart, not as data. An analyst reads those by eye, one at a time. This measures them instead, and says when it should not be trusted.

Why build it

What would make this worth using rather than a demo

It does things an analyst cannot do by hand

  • Measure 186 bars off a waterfall and count how many clear a threshold.
  • Read every figure in a paper the day it posts, for about thirty cents.
  • Re-measure everything when the key changes. That is how I found most apparent measurement error was the harness discarding correct values.

It is auditable

  • Every number traces to a pixel and an axis. The conversion is arithmetic, so it repeats exactly.
  • Confidence comes from structural checks that need no answer key, so the system can decline instead of guessing.
  • The key carries provenance per value: from the paper, derived, or unverified. 13 of 27 are unverified, and this says so.

A few days of work against six figures. It shows the shape of the thing, not something I would put in front of an analyst tomorrow.

Approach

General model first, specialization only where it fails

I put infrastructure around a general vision model first and held the specialized parts in reserve, rather than build a bespoke reader per chart type before knowing which needed one. It read the raster table perfectly and did well on the Kaplan-Meier and PK curves. On the waterfall, forest and spider it scored 2 of 6, 1 of 3 and 0 of 1: reliable when the number is printed, unreliable when it has to be measured.

So the specialized path uses the model only for the panels, the legend, what each reference line marks, the printed patient count. I locate those lines in the pixels, pair them with its values, and convert each bar tip through the resulting axis. That half is arithmetic, which is what makes a number traceable.

The evaluation

Most of what I fixed was the harness, not the readers

Almost none of the gain came from changing how anything is measured. It came from finding correct answers thrown away before scoring.

Correct measurements discarded on a key mismatch. The forest reader produced 0.50 against a published 0.50 and it was dropped, because it emitted hazard_ratio.idh wild type while the key said hazard_ratio.idh_wildtype. Nothing errored; the matcher scored the weaker read instead. A one-line assertion that every emitted key exists in the contract would catch it, and still does not exist.

A unit conversion nobody had written. "70%" was scored against a stored 0.80 as an error of 69 rather than 0.10. Fixing it took interval coverage from 0.5 to 1.0, a jump I had logged as a measurement regression.

Answers never scored at all. Three of four spider quantities were marked interpretive and excluded, so most of the work there was invisible. Scored against criteria taken verbatim from the brief, I was failing two of five.

Single runs do not mean what they look like. Spread inside one configuration, 0.19 by hand and 0.15 automated, exceeds the 0.085 between them. That caught me out: a forest improvement reported off one sample did not survive three repeats.

Calibration

Automated calibration matches hand everywhere but forest

Every reader is pointed at its figure by a calibration: which pixel row is probability 1.0, which column is hazard ratio 1. Those were typed by hand, so the readers carried over to a new deck and the calibration did not. Kaplan-Meier, forest and PK now derive their own, for $0.0294.

The whole gap is 11 values out of 130 and forest is exactly 11. Not a measurement problem: the builder labelled rows positionally, row0 through row21, while the key uses the printed subgroup names, so every computed forest value was thrown away. The calibration is sound. It read 0.46 (0.27-0.73) where the key says 0.50 (0.32-0.74), placing the axis at 362 and 730 pixels against hand values of 347 and 747. Same silent key drift as above, now twice.

The loop: deterministic measurement enumerates candidate axis positions and series colours, the vision step picks which carries which value, the choice is measured, and structural checks accept or reject. A rejection is fed back. The system is told what to look for, since the contract names the subgroups and landmarks. It is not told the answers.

Checks that earn their place

A hazard ratio interval cannot reach zero: the wrong calibration emitted 0.57 (-0.00-0.87) and was accepted twice, because the value is a formatted string and only its leading number was ever parsed. An axis spans its own plotted data, measured independently of anything the vision step nominated, which caught a proposal putting the horizontal axis across 21 pixels of a figure whose curves run 600. A survival curve starts at 1.0, probabilities stay in [0,1], concentrations are positive.

Three checks I built and then deleted

Each rejected correct answers, the more useful half of the exercise.

  • A curve-start check searching from the top of the page found grey title text matching a placebo arm, rejected a correct calibration, then steered the next two attempts away from the answer.
  • A check counting subgroups sharing an interval endpoint, on the theory that ties mean whiskers hitting a window edge. Ties at two decimals are ordinary across twenty rows, so it fired on the known-good calibration too.
  • A rule capping the vertical axis span at twice the data span. It cleared the known-good calibration by 26%, against 70% and 75% for the others.

A wrong check is worse than a missing one, because its verdict is fed back and steers the search away from the answer. Every check is now tested against a known-good calibration first. Two others, designed to be non-circular, turned out silent instead: both needed ticks found independently, and the detector finds plot frames and banners, not ticks.

Intended routing, not a measured property. It rests on disagreement between two reads predicting error, and across two runs that correlation came out r = 0.968 then r = −0.093. Validating that trigger is the biggest gap between this and something deployable.

Limits

Where it fails

In the system

  • Left edge truncation gives bad leftmost values and a 4% undercount, since sorting puts the deepest bars at the clipped edge.
  • Runs are not reproducible. Five hand-calibrated runs ranged 0.731 to 0.923, because the reader intermittently emits nothing for the table, once dropping eight values.
  • Density ceiling. At 186 bars the count is short by seven. Below about 60, counts land exactly.

In the evaluation

  • Half the key is a manual read. 14 of 27 values come from public sources. The other 13, including all three forest values, were never published, so those scores measure agreement with me.
  • Six figures is not evidence of generality. A held-out set matters more than another point of accuracy.
  • Confidence drifts the wrong way. Calibration error rose to 0.305 as accuracy improved, because low-confidence answers keep turning out correct.
Next

What I would build first

The key-contract assertion, a few lines that should close the 11-value gap. Then repeat counts on everything I report, since one run tells you nothing. Then a held-out set, because every parameter so far was tuned while watching six outputs. Not off-the-shelf chart models: a chart-to-table model returned all zeros on a nine-bar figure and truncated to 18 rows on a 186-bar one, and an open-vocabulary detector returned no boxes at all.

Tooling: Python, a hosted vision model for extraction and a smaller one for routing, pixel measurement in the standard library with no third-party dependencies. Public sources only, traced to source papers where stated and flagged unverified where not.
