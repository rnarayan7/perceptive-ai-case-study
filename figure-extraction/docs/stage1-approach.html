Stage 1 · Figure extraction

Extracting values from biotech charts

Every number that matters in a readout is locked inside a picture. Median PFS, hazard ratios by subgroup, how many patients shrank past 30 percent: published as a chart, not as data. An analyst reads those by eye, one at a time. This measures them instead, and says when it should not be trusted.

Why build it

What would make this worth using rather than a demo

It does things an analyst cannot do by hand

  • Measure 186 bars off a waterfall and count how many clear a threshold.
  • Read every figure in a paper the day it posts, for about thirty cents.
  • Re-measure everything when the key changes. That is how I found most apparent measurement error was the harness discarding correct values.

It is auditable

  • Every number traces to a pixel and an axis. The conversion is arithmetic, so it repeats exactly.
  • Confidence comes from structural checks that need no answer key, so the system can decline instead of guessing.
  • The key carries provenance per value: from the paper, derived, or unverified. 13 of 27 are unverified, and this says so.

A few days of work against six figures. It shows the shape of the thing, not something I would put in front of an analyst tomorrow.

Architecture

The model recognises, geometry measures

What the vision model does

  • Classifies the figure so the right reader runs. 5 of 6 correct; it sends the spider plot to the waterfall reader.
  • Finds structure: panels, legend, which series is which arm or dose.
  • Reads printed text: axis labels and the value each carries, what a reference line marks, the patient count in the caption.
  • Never returns a magnitude it had to measure. Its numbers are labels, not readings.

What deterministic CV does

  • Locates rules, ticks and plot edges as pixel rows and columns.
  • Traces each series by colour, finding bar tips, marker centres and whisker ends.
  • Converts pixel positions through the axis, linear or log, into values.
  • Never decides what a position means. It cannot tell a tick from a letter stem.

02

Calibrate. Two labelled positions become an axis. Candidates enumerated by geometry, chosen by the model, accepted or rejected by invariants.both, in a loop

Measure. Bar tips, curve heights, marker centres and whisker ends, converted through that axis.geometry
05
Verify. Structural invariants accept the reading or decline the figure.geometry
  • Why split it there. Asked to read values directly, the model scored 1.00 on the printed table and 2 of 6, 1 of 3 and 0 of 1 on the waterfall, forest and spider. Reliable on text, unreliable on magnitude.
  • Geometry is exact and repeats, but it cannot recognise. Nothing in the pixels says the rule at column 546 is the null line.
  • Each half only does the thing it cannot fail at, and when either one is wrong it shows up as a violated invariant rather than a plausible number.

Calibration

Automated calibration matches hand everywhere but forest

  • Every reader is pointed at its figure by a calibration: which pixel row is probability 1.0, which column is hazard ratio 1. Those were typed by hand, so the readers carried over to a new deck and the calibration did not.
  • Kaplan-Meier, forest and PK now derive their own, for $0.0294 total.

  • The gap is 11 values out of 130, and forest is all 11. Not a measurement problem: the builder labelled rows positionally, row0 through row21, while the key uses the printed subgroup names, so every computed forest value was discarded.
  • The forest calibration itself is sound. It read 0.46 (0.27-0.73) where the key says 0.50 (0.32-0.74), placing the axis at 362 and 730 pixels against hand values of 347 and 747. Same silent key drift as the earlier one, now twice.
  • The loop. Geometry enumerates candidate axis positions and series colours, the model picks which carries which value, the choice is measured, invariants accept or reject, and a rejection is fed back so the next attempt avoids it.
  • It is told what to look for, not what the answers are. The contract names which subgroups and landmarks to extract.

Checks that earn their place

  • A hazard ratio interval cannot reach zero. The wrong calibration emitted 0.57 (-0.00-0.87) and was accepted twice, because the value is a formatted string and only its leading number was ever parsed.
  • An axis spans its own plotted data, measured independently of anything the model nominated. This caught a proposal putting the horizontal axis across 21 pixels of a figure whose curves run 600.
  • A survival curve starts at 1.0, probabilities stay in [0,1], concentrations are positive.

Three checks I built and then deleted

Each rejected correct answers, the more useful half of the exercise.

  • A curve-start check searching from the top of the page found grey title text matching a placebo arm, rejected a correct calibration, then steered the next two attempts away from the answer.
  • A check counting subgroups sharing an interval endpoint, on the theory that ties mean whiskers hitting a window edge. Ties at two decimals are ordinary across twenty rows, so it fired on the known-good calibration too.
  • A rule capping the vertical axis span at twice the data span. It cleared the known-good calibration by 26%, against 70% and 75% for the others.

A wrong check is worse than a missing one, because its verdict is fed back and steers the search away from the answer. Every check is now tested against a known-good calibration first. Two others, designed to be non-circular, turned out silent instead: both needed ticks found independently, and the detector finds plot frames and banners, not ticks.

Limits

Where it fails

In the system

  • Left edge truncation gives bad leftmost values and a 4% undercount, since sorting puts the deepest bars at the clipped edge.
  • Runs are not reproducible. Five hand-calibrated runs ranged 0.731 to 0.923, because the reader intermittently emits nothing for the table, once dropping eight values.
  • Density ceiling. At 186 bars the count is short by seven. Below about 60, counts land exactly.

In the evaluation

  • Half the key is a manual read. 14 of 27 values come from public sources. The other 13, including all three forest values, were never published, so those scores measure agreement with me.
  • Six figures is not evidence of generality. A held-out set matters more than another point of accuracy.
  • Confidence drifts the wrong way. Calibration error rose to 0.305 as accuracy improved, because low-confidence answers keep turning out correct.
  • Single runs are noise. Spread inside one configuration, 0.19 by hand and 0.15 automated, exceeds the 0.085 between them. A forest improvement reported off one sample did not survive three repeats.
Next

What I would build first

  • The key-contract assertion. A few lines that should close the 11-value gap.
  • Repeat counts on everything reported, since one run tells you nothing.
  • A held-out set, because every parameter so far was tuned while watching six outputs.
  • Not off-the-shelf chart models. A chart-to-table model returned all zeros on a nine-bar figure and truncated to 18 rows on a 186-bar one; an open-vocabulary detector returned no boxes at all.
